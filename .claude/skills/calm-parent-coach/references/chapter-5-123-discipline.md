# No-Drama Discipline — Chapter 5: "1-2-3 Discipline" (plain-language summary)

Redirection = how we respond when kids do something we don't like (or don't do something we need). It comes **after** connecting. Goal: short-term cooperation now + long-term building of the child's thinking brain.

It's not a formula to memorize — just a checklist to keep in mind.

## 1 Definition: Discipline means TEACHING
- Not punishment. Punishing fast (yelling, sending away, taking things) shifts the child's focus to "my parent is mean," and they may even feel like the victim.
- Let the child feel the natural "uh-oh" guilt. Healthy guilt = a working conscience. When we help them notice that feeling, it becomes their inner compass — it works even when no one is watching.

**Crayon story:** A 6-year-old slipped crayons into her pocket at a store. Mom didn't yell. She knelt down and asked, "Can you tell me about these?" She asked simple questions: "What's it called when you take something that isn't yours?" "The store lady paid money for those — did you know?" The girl cried. Mom just held her, didn't rush it. Then they talked about honesty and made a plan to return the crayons.

## 2 Principles

### Principle 1: Wait until your child is ready (and you are too)
- Right after misbehavior is often the WORST time to teach — the thinking brain is offline.
- Ask: "Is my child ready to hear, learn, understand?" If no → more connection, or time and space.
- Too sleepy/checked-out OR too wound-up/anxious = can't learn. Calm and alert = the sweet spot.
- It's OK to wait until later today or tomorrow. Kids 3+ remember. "Let's talk about what happened yesterday at bedtime."
- Ask: "Am I ready?" If not: "I'm too angry to talk well right now. I'll calm down, then we'll talk."

### Principle 2: Be consistent, but not rigid
- **Consistent** = a steady approach so kids know what to expect. Limits are like guardrails on a bridge — they make kids feel safe.
- **Rigid** = following a rule no matter what, often out of fear ("one soda and they'll drink it forever").
- Safety is non-negotiable. Other rules can flex for good reasons.
  - Rigid: "Grandparents are here, but the rule is homework first."
  - Flexible: "Grandparents are here, so homework can wait. When's a good time to do it?"
- **Do-overs:** "Want to try saying that again more respectfully?" Practice, like a soccer coach, not a traffic cop handing out tickets.
- **Creative repair:** "Sorry" doesn't fix a broken toy. An apology note + using allowance for a new one teaches more.

## 3 Outcomes (Mindsight: me, you, we)
Ask questions instead of lecturing.
1. **Insight (me):** understand their own feelings.
   - Little kids: name it. "When she took the doll, you looked really mad. Right?"
   - Older kids: "It looked like you got more and more annoyed before you blew up. Is that what happened?"
2. **Empathy (you):** see how others feel.
   - "See her tears? How do you think she feels?"
   - "Did you see his face when you yelled? He really looks up to you."
3. **Repair / integration (we):** make it right.
   - "What can you do to make it right?" "What do you think needs to happen now?"
   - Shy or little kids may need help. A parent can even deliver the apology together with them. Don't force a fake apology.

## Example: Roger & Ali (sibling left out)
9-year-old Ali took over her little sister Katie's playdate friend. Instead of just ordering "Give them space," Dad sat with her, arm around her, and asked:
- "Did you notice Katie's not too happy?" (Ali got defensive: "I'm not doing anything mean!")
- "I didn't say you did anything wrong. I'm asking if you noticed how she feels." → "I guess."
- "Why might she be upset?" / "If she were here, what would she say?" → "That it's her playdate, not mine."
- Then: ask Ali to help make a plan ("One more song, then I'll go get ready").
- If it hadn't worked, Dad could just make the call or play a game with all three. Either way, Ali got practice thinking about others.

## Example: Naila's phone (12-year-old)
Mom caught her on the phone after lights-out (and Dad had too, the week before).
- **In the moment:** connect ("I hate not being able to sleep too"), take the phone, "Let's talk tomorrow." No late-night lecture.
- **Lesson:** honesty, responsibility, trust.
- **Not rigid:** they considered taking the phone away completely but saw she'd been responsible otherwise. Instead they made new rules together, plus a clear next step if it happened again (parents hold the phone except at set times).
- **Insight:** "How do you feel inside when you do something you know you shouldn't?" "Next time you can't sleep, what could you do instead?"
- **Empathy:** no guilt trips — just honestly said trust got a little dented.
- **Repair:** "What could you do to help us feel good about trusting you?" (e.g. leave the phone outside the room without being asked).

## When to get help
If reactions are intense, frequent, long-lasting, and don't respond to repair, talk to a pediatric therapist or child-development specialist. Possible factors: sensory issues, attention/impulsivity (ADHD), mood, past trauma, or a parent-child temperament mismatch.
